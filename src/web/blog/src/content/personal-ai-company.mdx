Running a company on your own means doing work you haven't had to do before, often with nobody to ask. Even if you know your trade, there are only so many hours to learn everything else. Hiring a full team may be out of reach.

A few AI agents seem like a way to get help. Then you find yourself explaining the same background in different chats, copying one agent's answer to another, and checking whether anyone picked up the next step. You still have several jobs on your desk, including managing the agents.

For your own agent company, the practical goal is a team that picks up each other's work and shows you what still needs a decision. The roles and customer job in this guide give you a starting point, including setup in a [multi agent collaboration workspace](https://alook.ai).

## What Should Your Team Know About Your Business?

Suppose you run a website-maintenance service for local shops, helping customers change opening hours and fix broken links on existing sites.

A shop asking for a new website may sound like a promising lead, but that's a different service. If Sales treats maintenance and a redesign alike, you could end up discussing work you haven't priced or planned to deliver.

The **company brief** gives Sales a place to check what you offer and when a request needs your attention. It also explains which customers the service is for. A **job brief** then holds one customer's agreed work, such as the pages to change, the correct hours, and the booking URL. You can reuse the company brief with a new agent. The customer's hours and booking URL stay with that customer's job.

## How Many Agents Do You Need to Start?

For this example, our starting recommendation is four: Sales, Delivery, Review, and Customer Ops. Finding new work and following up on an existing job pull attention in different directions. A website change also needs someone to check it.

Review is the role we're most reluctant to fold into Delivery. If Review relies on Delivery's summary, it may accept the very mistake it was meant to catch. A separate reviewer compares the customer's request with the actual preview.

Four isn't a minimum for every business. Early on, Sales and Customer Ops might share one agent. Prospect research holding up replies to existing customers is a reason to split them. Adding another agent just to make the company look bigger isn't.

## How to Structure AI Agents Into Departments and Roles

### Give Each Role Something You Can Check

“Sales” might mean researching prospects or promising a price. Asking what the agent will bring back gives you a chance to catch that misunderstanding before a customer is involved.

Sales starts with prospect research and incoming inquiries. If research delays replies to people who already want to buy, we'd separate Prospecting from Intake. Prospecting's lead notes give Intake the background for handling inquiries and preparing job briefs. Both still report to you. One summarizes their sales work for Customer Ops, so you don't end up with another pair of chats to check each morning.

### Reporting Lines and Direct Handoffs

All four agents report to you, but their work doesn't need to pass through your hands at every step:

**Sales → Delivery → Review → Customer Ops**

When Review finds a broken link, Delivery needs that finding without waiting for you to copy it across. Customer Ops brings progress and questions together, saving you four separate requests for updates.

Promises to customers and requests for more work need particular care. Customer Ops can pass a correction to Delivery, but a new page goes back to Sales for a proposal. You decide the price and any change in scope. A passing review also doesn't mean the site goes live: publishing, invoices, and payment decisions still wait for you.

A vague “continue” can leave two agents waiting for each other. Naming the next agent and the work to pick up settles that question. More complex [agent orchestration](/blog/ai-agent-orchestration) still depends on that basic clarity.

### Set Each Agent's Tool Access

Delivery needs a place to prepare changes, with a preview Review can open. Neither needs permission to publish just to do those jobs. A broad permission could let an agent change the live site while you're still checking its work.

For Sales and Customer Ops, drafting a customer reply doesn't require sending it. Their instructions need to say which accounts they may use and whether you're reviewing messages first. The accounts and tools need to enforce those permissions. Writing “don't publish” in a role description won't remove access to the publish button.

## Run a Customer Job Through the Team

### Sales Hands Over the Job Brief

A shop asks for new opening hours and a repaired booking link, but hasn't sent the replacement URL. That's worth catching in Sales's proposed job brief. Otherwise, Delivery may spend time guessing which booking page is right.

Sales passes your approved brief to Delivery. A quick reply from Delivery tells the others it's on the job. If the customer changes the hours later, the team needs the new version of the job brief, not another loose instruction buried in chat.

We've run into this version problem in our own article workflow. We send the draft and its version to a reviewing agent, but we've still had feedback refer to text we'd already changed. We had to check which draft the comments referred to before deciding what still needed fixing. The same problem arises when a customer's latest request differs from the one Delivery first saw.

### Send the Website Preview for Review

![Six handoff requirements: scope, input, output, completion criteria, failure path, and approval boundary](/blog/personal-ai-company/handoff-contract.webp)

For Delivery, the shop's job brief makes the work and expected return specific:

<CompanyJobBrief>

| Field | This customer's job |
|---|---|
| Scope | Change the hours and booking link; keep the existing design |
| Input | Affected pages, correct hours, and confirmed booking URL |
| Output | Preview link, change list, and results of checking the hours and booking link |
| Completion | Each requested change is ready to check, or the team has said exactly what's missing |
| Failure | Ask Customer Ops for missing details or help with access; keep the work already done and bring scope questions to you |
| Approval | Prepare and revise the preview; publish only after you agree |

</CompanyJobBrief>

Review has to open that preview. The booking link needs to open the URL in the job brief. A summary saying “link fixed” isn't enough to establish that.

“Looks wrong” leaves Delivery with another guessing job. A useful reply is: “The booking link still opens the old destination. Use the URL in job brief v2, then return the updated preview and link-check result.” The next review can check that same link again.

### When the Job Gets Stuck

A missing booking URL doesn't make the hours change impossible. Delivery prepares that part and tells Customer Ops: “Hours ready in preview; booking link waiting for the confirmed destination.” Customer Ops has a specific question for the customer. If it isn't allowed to send customer messages yet, the question waits for you to send it.

With the URL confirmed and the change reviewed, Customer Ops brings you the preview and findings for approval. The customer might still spot a wrong hour afterward; that correction needs another check. A new page is a different conversation about price and scope.

## Keep Customer Jobs Moving

With two shops waiting, a reply or booking URL could get used for the wrong customer. Each job needs its own brief and conversation, and the agent picking it up needs to read that customer's history.

The daily view we want from Customer Ops is short: inquiries waiting for a price, previews ready for approval, jobs waiting for information, and overdue follow-ups. You can choose what needs attention without reading every exchange. Delivery and Review can keep working on agreed tasks while a separate pricing question waits.

Sometimes work waits for you. Customer Ops needs to keep a preview awaiting your approval visible, so a quiet conversation doesn't look like a finished job.

## Is the Team Saving You Time?

The first signal we look for is how often the work comes back to you for an explanation you've already given. If Delivery still needs you to reconstruct the customer's request, adding more agents won't solve that handoff.

Time spent checking, correcting, and following up counts alongside time doing the website work. A few completed jobs tell you more than a fast first draft. Model and tool costs count too. Without earlier records, the next few jobs give you a baseline before you decide the team saves time.

An agent that repeatedly returns work you can't use may need a [narrower task](/blog/how-to-delegate-tasks-to-ai-agents). Adding a role before that is clear gives you another agent to supervise.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Does “Zero-Person Company” Mean Here?

We're talking about one human owner running a business with agents. “Zero-person company” or “0-person company” can overstate how much you can leave to them. In this example, you still approve prices, changes in scope, and publishing.

### How Is This Different From Claude Code Agent Teams?

Claude Code agent teams is a specific feature for coordinating several Claude Code sessions, with a lead, shared tasks, and messaging. For the company in this guide, you choose business roles and tools; the agents don't all have to use Claude Code.
