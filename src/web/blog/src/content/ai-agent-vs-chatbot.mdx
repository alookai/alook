A chatbot gives you a back-and-forth conversation with AI. An AI agent works toward a goal, deciding what to try next based on what it finds along the way. The two overlap in practice: plenty of agents live inside a chat window, and plenty of chatbots can call tools. What actually separates them is how the work moves forward.

If you want a sounding board while you figure out a reply, a normal chat is usually plenty. Once you ask AI to check your systems, weigh what it finds, and assemble a draft without you steering each step, you are asking it to act like an agent.

## The same task, two ways to handle it

Say someone emails asking to push a meeting to next week, and you haven't looked at your schedule yet.

In a chatbot, you paste the email and ask for reply options. The model gives you three ways to phrase it. You check your calendar yourself, pick Tuesday afternoon, ask for a friendlier tone, and copy the text into your mail app. That loop works well whenever you are still figuring out what you want to say, such as holding off on the meeting until another deal closes.

With an agent, you grant read access to that email thread and your calendar, then ask it to find an open afternoon next week and leave a draft reply for review. The agent checks the meeting duration, scans next week's slots, and decides from what it sees whether to draft a concrete proposal or flag a scheduling conflict for you. You define the goal and boundaries upfront rather than prompting every step.

Notice that both paths can stop at an unsent draft. Letting AI hit send is a separate permission decision; an agent is still an agent when it stops for human review.

Separating autonomy from write permissions matters the moment the AI guesses wrong. If an unsent draft suggests a bad time, you fix the line in two seconds and nobody ever knows. If the agent had permission to send the email automatically, the other person is already rearranging their week around a mistake you now have to walk back.

Chatbots can still give bad advice that trips you up, of course. With an agent, though, you also have to audit what state it can change before you ever see the output. "Draft a reply" and "send a reply" carry very different blast radii.

## Who chooses the next step?

Calling a calendar API doesn't automatically make something an agent. A deterministic script can parse an incoming email, query free calendar slots, and fill out a template. Even with `if/else` branches for busy weeks, a script only follows paths a developer hardcoded ahead of time.

An agent chooses its next move dynamically from what it encounters. Suppose the sender asks for a full hour, and next week only has 30-minute gaps. Should the agent look two weeks out, offer a 30-minute call, or ask you how to handle it? Navigating that kind of judgment call within your instructions is the actual work you delegate to an agent.

How the task gets triggered doesn't change that distinction. Whether you kick the job off with a chat message or run it on a scheduled timer, one agent is still choosing and executing the steps.

To finish the job coherently, the agent does need working context for the task in front of it: which email it is answering, which calendar days it already checked, and what actions are off-limits. It can hold onto that state for the current run even if every new task starts clean. Long-term memory about your meeting habits is nice to have later, though unnecessary for finishing today's draft.

Marketing labels won't tell you which mode you are using either. Inside ChatGPT, you might bounce phrasing ideas back and forth in one turn and hand off a ten-step research job in the next, so calling the whole product "just a chatbot" misses what changed. On any real task, look at two things: how many intermediate choices you hand over, and what external actions the tool is allowed to take.

## When is a chatbot enough, and when do you need an agent?

Stick with a plain chat whenever you are still thinking through the decision itself. Maybe you want to weigh whether the meeting is worth taking, test a polite decline, or talk through politics with a coworker. Those conversational turns help you figure out what you actually want.

Reach for an agent when the goal is clear and you want to skip babysitting the lookup and assembly steps. Checking calendar availability and staging a draft fits that bill even as a one-off request, and if similar emails show up every week, reusing those instructions saves real tedium.

Ask two questions before handing a task over:

1. **Which intermediate choices do I want to make myself, and which can the AI make on its own?**
2. **If it makes a mistake mid-run, what has it already saved, changed, or sent?**

On that meeting email, letting an agent check your calendar and stage a draft while keeping the Send button for yourself hits a practical sweet spot. Writing a clear [task brief for an AI agent](/blog/how-to-delegate-tasks-to-ai-agents) builds that stopping point right into the assignment.
