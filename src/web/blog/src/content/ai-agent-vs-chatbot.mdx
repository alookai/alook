A chatbot gives you a way to talk with AI. An AI agent can work toward a goal, choosing what to do next as it gets results. They can overlap: an agent may work through a chat window, and a chatbot may have tools. The useful distinction is how the work moves forward.

If you want help thinking through a reply, conversation may be all you need. If you want AI to investigate the situation and prepare the reply without asking you to direct every step, you're asking it to take on more of the job.

## The same task, two ways to handle it

Suppose someone emails to ask whether you can move a meeting to next week. You haven't decided what to offer.

In a chat, you could paste the message and ask for a reply. The AI might suggest a few ways to phrase it. You choose a time, ask for a warmer tone, and copy the draft into your email. That back-and-forth is useful when the decision is still yours to make. You may know, for example, that this meeting should wait until another conversation has happened.

Now imagine giving an agent access to the relevant email and calendar, with instructions to find a free afternoon next week and prepare a reply for review. It could check the meeting length, compare available slots, and use what it finds to decide whether to draft a proposal or ask you about a conflict. You set the goal and constraints; you don't have to tell it each next move.

Both routes can end with an unsent draft. Sending is a separate permission, not the feature that makes the second route an agent.

That distinction matters when something goes wrong. If the draft offers a time you dislike, you can change it before anyone else sees it. If the system has permission to send and offers the wrong time, the other person may already be planning around it. The wrong time is now a commitment you need to correct.

A chatbot can still give advice that leads you to a bad decision. With an agent, you also need to know which changes it can make before you read the result. For this email, “prepare a reply” and “send a reply” are two different jobs.

## Who chooses the next step?

Checking a calendar doesn't settle whether a system is an agent. A prearranged process could read an email, look up free slots, and fill in a reply template. It could handle a full calendar differently from an empty one while still following rules that someone wrote in advance.

The agent's role is to choose its next step from what it finds. If the sender asks for an hour but only half-hour slots are free, should it look at another day, propose a shorter meeting, or ask you? The answer depends on the instructions and room for judgment you've given it. That's the part of the task you're delegating.

You can start this job yourself and let one agent carry out several steps. A timer is one way to start it; a message from you works too.

It does need enough context to follow the current task: which email it's answering, which slots it checked, and what you allowed it to do. It can carry those details through this job even if it starts fresh on the next one. Remembering your preferences for future meetings would be an additional convenience.

Product names won't reliably tell you which arrangement you're using, either. ChatGPT can help you think through a reply or carry out a task through several steps; treating the entire product as “just a chatbot” misses that difference. For the meeting email, the difference is how much of the job you hand over and what the system is allowed to do with it.

## When is a chatbot enough, and when do you need an agent?

Stay with conversation when you want to explore the decision. You may need to explain why the meeting matters, try several replies, or decide whether to accept it at all. Those turns aren't wasted effort. They are where you work out what you want.

An agent becomes useful when you can hand over the goal but don't want to manage every step needed to reach it. Finding suitable slots and preparing a reply can be one such task, even if you only do it once. If the job comes up again, the same instructions may also save you another round of explaining.

Two questions help make that choice concrete:

1. **Which next steps do I want to choose, and which can the AI choose for me?**
2. **If it gets something wrong, what will already have been saved, changed, or sent?**

For the meeting email, you might let the agent investigate availability and prepare the message while keeping the send decision. You get help with the work without handing over the final commitment.
